The B+1 m/z is the charge determinant — z = round(1.0034 ÷ (B+1 − B0)), which works for every charge state, not just 2+. Its exact mass also names the element: ¹³C, ²⁹Si, ³³S, ⁵³Cr and ⁵⁷Fe all sit at different offsets near +1.

⁵⁴Fe, ⁵⁰Cr, ¹⁰B and ⁶Li all put intensity below the base peak, and titanium puts it on both sides. Metals are only called on the satellite pair, never on one peak alone.

On a unit-resolution instrument an accurate mass is not available, so matching falls back to ±0.5 Da on the nominal mass and the isotope offsets (¹³C at +1.0034 vs ⁵³Cr at +1.0001) can no longer be told apart — those are 3 mDa apart and your instrument cannot resolve them. Expect many more candidates; use retention time, the isotope ratio and the ladder spacing to choose between them.

How this works

Enter the m/z of the peak you cannot explain — the tallest peak of the cluster, not the one you think is monoisotopic. For iron the most abundant isotope is not the lightest, and past about ninety carbons the ¹³C peak overtakes the ¹²C one, so "which peak is monoisotopic" is ambiguous exactly where it matters. The tallest peak never is.

Four layers run in order, and every one of them reports its own evidence: the library of — known contaminant ions covering — compounds; mobile-phase clusters generated combinatorially from the solvents you say were flowing; formula generation constrained by the isotope peaks you measured; and companion logic — the sodiated partner, the cluster ladder, the dimer, the water loss — which is what actually separates a molecule from a solvent cluster.

Isobaric alternatives are always shown side by side. Mass alone cannot separate trifluoroacetate from a sodium formate cluster, and a tool that silently picks one is lying to you.

🔒 Everything runs locally in your browser. Nothing you type or paste is sent anywhere, which matters when peak lists are confidential.

Why LC-MS contaminants matter

Background ions from plastics, solvents, tubing and labware routinely dominate LC-MS blanks, suppress real analytes and get mistaken for biological features. Tracing one to its source can take weeks — a siloxane at m/z 371.1012 might come from vial septa, ambient laboratory air, or a spent carbon filter on a nitrogen generator. This tool exists to shorten that search.

Aggregated from peer-reviewed contaminant tables, instrument-vendor background ion lists, core-facility tables, spectral databases and public code repositories. 3,842 of 4,973 ions (77%) have their m/z recomputed here from a molecular formula and adduct; 1,004 carry the value their source published, and 127 only a nominal integer mass. The last two are labelled on every result, and the nominal ones are shown as ~45 rather than as an exact mass. About 38% of entries rest on domain knowledge cross-checked against this literature rather than on a single citable table.
